## Your accent is fine

Everyone has an accent, native speakers included. Someone from Scotland or Texas sounds different from someone from Kenya or Nigeria, and English works for all of them. In fact, about three in four people who speak English learned it as a second language, just like you ([Ethnologue, 2026](https://www.ethnologue.com/insights/most-spoken-language/)).

A Rwandan accent is fine too: it tells people you speak more than one language. This guide does not try to replace your accent. It only works on the few sounds that can change a word's meaning, so listeners hear the word you meant.

Research backs this up. Munro and Derwing found that a strong foreign accent does not necessarily make speech harder to understand ([Language Learning, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x)). The six rules give you focused practice with sounds and word stress that can help listeners distinguish words and numbers.

> Communication can be remarkably successful when foreign accents are noticeable or even strong … there is no clear correlation between accent and understanding.
>
> — John Levis, applied linguist ([TESOL Quarterly, 2005](https://doi.org/10.2307/3588485))

## Why we wrote this guide

In Rwanda, we have seen a number of people lose clarity by trying to imitate an accent, in most cases the American one. The trouble comes from a few American sounds that collide with a different Kinyarwanda sound. The American tapped *t* is so close to the Kinyarwanda *r* that, when a Kinyarwanda speaker copies it, it comes out as an *r*: writing turns into riding, and better into 'berrer' or 'beller'. The American *r* after a vowel adds one more *r*, which listeners can hear as *l*. The six rules steer you away from these collisions, so you can stay clear without imitating anyone.

## A few sounds matter most

The linguist Jennifer Jenkins studied which sounds matter when people from different countries speak English to each other. One of them is a full *t* between vowels, as in water and better ([The Phonology of English as an International Language, 2000](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/the-phonology-of-english-as-an-international-language-new-models-/)). A 2022 study tested this with 78 learners of English: when the *t* was tapped, as it often is in American English, most words became harder for them to understand ([Social Sciences & Humanities Open](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssaho.2022.100313)). This matches what we have seen in Rwanda. See [Why Standard English](/guide/standard-english).
