## Your accent is fine

Everyone has an accent, and a Rwandan accent is fine.

Research backs this up. Munro and Derwing found that a strong foreign accent does not necessarily make speech harder to understand ([Language Learning, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x)). The six rules give you focused practice with sounds and word stress that can help listeners distinguish words and numbers.

> Communication can be remarkably successful when foreign accents are noticeable or even strong … there is no clear correlation between accent and understanding.
>
> — John Levis, applied linguist ([TESOL Quarterly, 2005](https://doi.org/10.2307/3588485))

## A few sounds matter most

The linguist Jennifer Jenkins studied which sounds matter when people from different countries speak English to each other. One of them is a full t between vowels, as in water and better ([The Phonology of English as an International Language, 2000](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/the-phonology-of-english-as-an-international-language-new-models-/)). A 2022 study tested this with 78 learners of English: when the t was tapped, as it often is in American English, most words became harder for them to understand ([Social Sciences & Humanities Open](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssaho.2022.100313)). The study did not include Kinyarwanda speakers, and it does not show that an American accent is hard to understand. Its findings support practising a full t in those listening conditions; our choice for this guide also draws on our training experience.

## What 'Standard English' means

In this guide, Standard English names the pronunciation model we practise. We have chosen these sounds through our training with Kinyarwanda speakers, building on familiar sounds to help you speak clearly and confidently. Follow the examples and recordings as you work through the six rules.

## Why we start from Standard English

In our training, we have found this model a practical starting point. Familiar sounds give learners something to build on, while focused practice helps with less familiar contrasts. Learners progress at different speeds.

- **A clear t.** The full t we practise here is close to the t you already use in Kinyarwanda. Start with water, better and twenty: listen, then repeat with clear tongue contact. See [Rule 1](/rules/say-every-t).
- **Words that end in a vowel.** In our model, car is kaa and computer ends in tə. These vowel endings offer a familiar starting point for Kinyarwanda speakers. Practise the examples, including the r that links to a following vowel. See [Rule 4](/rules/drop-the-r).
- **A starting point for o.** Use the Kinyarwanda o as a starting point for hot, job and stop, then match the recording. The sound spelling is a learning aid; listen for the difference from hut, nut and cup. See [Rule 5](/rules/short-o).

Practise the Standard English form shown here. Listen to the American example so you can recognise another pronunciation of the same word. Keeping r and l apart and distinguishing -teen from -ty remain useful in both styles.
