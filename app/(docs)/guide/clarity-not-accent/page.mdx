## Your accent is fine

Everyone has an accent, and a Rwandan accent is fine.

Research backs this up. Munro and Derwing found that a strong foreign accent does not necessarily make speech harder to understand ([Language Learning, 1995](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1467-1770.1995.tb00963.x)). What causes misunderstandings is a few specific sounds, and those are what the six rules fix.

> Communication can be remarkably successful when foreign accents are noticeable or even strong … there is no clear correlation between accent and understanding.
>
> — John Levis, applied linguist ([TESOL Quarterly, 2005](https://doi.org/10.2307/3588485))

## A few sounds matter most

The linguist Jennifer Jenkins studied which sounds matter when people from different countries speak English to each other. One of them is a full t between vowels, as in water and better ([The Phonology of English as an International Language, 2000](https://kclpure.kcl.ac.uk/portal/en/publications/the-phonology-of-english-as-an-international-language-new-models-/)). A 2022 study tested this with 78 learners of English: when the t was tapped, as it often is in American English, most words became harder for them to understand ([Social Sciences & Humanities Open](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.ssaho.2022.100313)).

## What 'Standard English' means

It is the pronunciation used in international English and in standard British English: the English of BBC World Service newsreaders and of the 'UK' audio in learner's dictionaries. It is not a London accent or a British street accent: those drop the t in water ('wa'er'), the opposite of [Rule 1](/rules/say-every-t).

## Why Standard English is easier for Kinyarwanda speakers

American English is correct for Americans, but three of its sounds are hard for a Kinyarwanda speaker to copy without being misunderstood. The Standard English versions are closer to sounds you already make:

- **A clear t.** The American t in water and better is tapped, and a tapped t is almost exactly the Kinyarwanda r, so listeners hear 'berrer' or 'beller', and writing as riding. The Standard English t stops the air completely, and nobody mistakes it for r. See [Rule 1](/rules/say-every-t).
- **Words that end in a vowel.** Standard English drops the r after a vowel: car is kaa, and computer ends in tə. Kinyarwanda syllables also end in vowels, so this comes naturally. American English keeps an r there, one more place where your r can be heard as l. See [Rule 4](/rules/drop-the-r).
- **The Kinyarwanda o.** Standard English says hot, job and stop with the Kinyarwanda o. The American vowel is the a you use for hut, held longer, so hot can be heard as hut or heart. See [Rule 5](/rules/short-o).

Keeping r and l apart and the stress in numbers work the same way in both styles.
